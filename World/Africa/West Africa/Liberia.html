Present-day residents migrated from the grassland area of the former Mali kingdom down to the coastal area between the 1500s and 1800s. Mande-speaking groups (including Kpelle, Vai, Loma, Belle, Gbandi, Gio, Dan, etc.) are said to have had the most dramatic impact on the Western Forest subregion.Mende institutions of Poro and warfare are seen as key conveyors of influence to Gola and, via Gola, to Vai and Dei.

Kwa speakers live in Liberia (Dei, Bassa, Gbi, Kran, Padebu, Kru, Grebo, Jabo); the Kwa in Liberia have felt heavy influence from migrants who have become dominant in the region.

Tukulor, Fulɓe, Wolof, Serer, and Jola (Senegambians) related peoples have migrated to Sierra Leone and Liberia, adding West Atlantic elements to the Liberian population.

Migrations from the north continuing until the 1800s resulted in the invention of an indigenous alphabet among the VAI and in the secret societies Poro and Sande, described as vehicles for the transmission of culture from one generation to the next in the Western Forest area that includes Liberia.

Early 19th-c. slave owners and idealistic African American missionaries in the US create the American Colonization Company to establish a colony in West Africa and “send black Americans back” while “civilizing” local Africans, just before the American Civil War. The colony is set up in 1822 and becomes Liberia (“free country”) in 1847. The African American settlers (“Americo-Liberians”) quickly form an elite whose culture clashes with that of local populations and who, for a time, trade people from the interior as slaves. This settler elite dominates politics after independence, forming the background against which later musical developments unfold. Indigenous rituals, Christianity and Islam coexist. Church choir music is introduced by settler ministers in the nineteenth century and becomes a key Liberian style.

Lower-class coastal KRU sailors communities formed the social base for PALM-WINE guitar-based dance and entertainment musics (syncretic fusion of European, Caribbean, Afro-American, and indigenous West African elements) performed informally in seaport taverns in the early twentieth century (prototypical HIGHLIFE genre). “Palm-wine highlife” groups proliferated from the 1930s to the early 1950s, extending this style inland and into minstrel and dramatic troupes. Early 1930s wax-cylinder recordings of music on the southeastern coast made by George Herzog.

The 1950s mark the first recordings of Liberian singers, still in a relatively stable pre-war political climate. A secular choral movement emerges in the 1950s (?40s?) with The Greenwood Singers, paralleling the long-established religious choirs rooted in the settler churches. Blind pianist Howard B. Hayes writes and plays catchy tunes in many idioms, including palm wine, calypso (Caribbean) and ragtime-like pieces. The 1954 recording "Songs of the African Coast: Café Music of Liberia and Ghana" mix calypso and ragtime-like rumbas with distinctly local parlance and humour, focusing on everyday life themes.

In 1970s artists like Morris Dorley and his Sunset Boys and Anthony Senyon Nagbe help “kick-start a real African music scene” with songs rooted in local realities and indigenous languages. Army musicians Robert Toe and Jimmy Diggs record cassettes that mix local and international material. T. Kpan Nimely and Donald Cooper develop a hybrid sound that blends reggae and highlife with Afro-rock and “discolypso”, described as a disco/funk version of Sierra Leone’s popular MARINGA music. Gospel singer Hawa Daisy Moore starts her career, than joins Miriam Makeba's band. E. Tonieh Williams’s band Liberian Dream, with Zack Roberts and Gebah Swaray (Zack and Gebah), performs what they call “Afro-Disco Music”, adding another hybrid dance style. Afro-jazz band Kapingbdi, founded by Ebenezer Kojo Samuels, tours Africa, Europe and the US, representing a fusion of jazz and African idioms. 1972 album "Music of the Kpelle of Liberia" demonstrates KPELLE influence in the area. Princess Fatu Gayflor work bridges pre-war cultural performance and diaspora life. KingBlack Hayes released his first hit, "Don’t Play Foul", in 1989.

End of 1989, Charles Taylor enters Liberia with armed men, starting the first civil war and closing this pre-war musical phase; hundreds of thousands flee and many musicians, like Yatta Zoe, go into exile, and studios and radio stations are looted, destroyed and burned. Zack Roberts, SwaRay (Gebah & Maudline Swaray) and Miatta Fahnbulleh form a repertoire of anti-war, refugee-focused music.

A second civil war (2000–03) pushes many prominent musicians abroad, deepening diaspora musical production. Gospel and choral traditions remain strong with groups like African Gospel a Cappella. Refugee rap/hip-hop emerges with The Boyz of Butuburam. Naser Sokay & Jake D move to the U.S.A.. After wars, local music starts “coming back”, increasingly drawing on Liberia’s interior, which had long been isolated. Musicians face heavy competition from US artists, ever-present reggae, mass-produced Congolese NDOMBOLO and the newer craze, ZOUGLOU from neighboring Côte d’Ivoire. Liberia is described as “cassette country”, and radio DJs help bring local tracks by artists like Zack Roberts and King O’Bryant.
