Burkina Faso is explicitly listed among West African countries in the savanna/forest belt, situating it within the larger Sahel–Guinea savanna context of agricultural development and north–south migration.Voltaic cluster peoples (Mossi-Bariba, Senufo, Dogon, Kasena-Nankani, LoDagaa) are dispersed within Burkina Faso and neighboring states, so many of the region’s political and social histories (kingdoms, confederacies, secret societies, chieftaincies) are shared across current borders.

Is described as a country of about sixty ethnic groups, with west and southwest under the influence of Mande culture shared with Mali and Côte d’Ivoire; DYULA people have their capital at Bobo-Dioulasso, a strategic commercial and cultural center whose strongest traditions are those of balafon and percussion. Central Burkina is inhabited mainly by Mossi (about half the population); the north is home to Fulbe (Fula/Peul), Bella and Touareg (linked to Mali and Niger); the south, along borders with Côte d’Ivoire, Benin and Ghana, includes Dyula, Senoufo, Gourounsi, Bissa, and Nankana peoples, all with strong musical traditions.

Regional BALAFON and percussion-based traditional musics dominate west and southwest Burkina Faso, within the broader Mande cultural sphere shared with Mali and Côte d’Ivoire.

1950s regional orchestras play foxtrots, waltzes and tangos specifically for colonial administrators, signalling strong European dance influence that offer Dyula musicians a different kind of ensemble music alongside jaliyaa and dance drumming.

- The 1984 revolution of Thomas Sankara renames the country from Upper Volta to Burkina Faso, a political turning point explicitly linked to the redefinition of national identity in discussions of cultural diversity.

Field-recording albums such as “Burkina Faso - Bisa, Gan, Lobi, Mossi, Savannah Rhythms, La Voix Des Peuls” and “Rhythms of the Grasslands” document representative traditional musics of major ethnic groups.

*****Numerous musicians continue Mossi traditions for primarily Mossi audiences; Zoubna Zanda can fill a stadium without publicity, showing the strength of local celebrity within ethnic communities.

An earlier generation of popular artists like Maurice Semporé, Oger Kaboré, Georges Ouédraogo, Tall Mountaga and Black So Man is noted for rich output but limited recognition outside Burkina Faso, marking a cohort whose fame remains largely domestic. Veteran singer-songwriter Gabin Dabiré, based in Italy since the mid-1970s, gains recognition for GUITAR based songs and complex choral arrangements, extending Burkinabé song into European world-music circuits.

- Mid-1980s onward: groups like Farafina, Badenya Les Frères Coulibaly, Djiguiya, Kady Diarra and Sabouyouma begin touring Europe and America, inserting Burkinabé ensemble music and balafon tradition into international circuits.

1989: Bazar Music, founded by Kaboré Moussa, pioneers the local recording industry focused on traditional music; labels such as Sika Sound, Africa Musique and Faso Ambiance follow, making traditional music available on cassette and later CD.

- 1996: Nuits Atypiques, a non-competitive international festival mixing traditional and modern acts, is launched in Koudougou by Koudbi Koala (founder of dance/percussion La Troupe Saaba) with support from French and Dutch world-music festivals.

Burkinabé musicians like Zêdess, Jean Claude Bamogo and Solo Dja Kabako record some REGGAE alongside other genres.

The compilation “Le Gang Rebel du Faso” features young artists such as Jah Verity, Razo Star, Terry Yiriban, Founy Faya (African-style SKA) Awa Melone (easy-listening), Sana Bob, Z. Mo, (tradi-moderne blends) and Les Sœurs Doga (purely traditional music).

RAP / HIP-HOP becomes the most popular youth genre; the Ouaga Hip Hop Festival and media support foster an energetic local scene, and Ouagadougou is described as one of West Africa’s major trendsetters in hip-hop. Hip-hop artists such as Yeleen, Smockey, Sofaa, Kas Boven, Kouman Kan, Tere Pirattack and especially Faso Kombat become stars of the media-backed rap scene. Madson Junior, a ten-year-old rapper, wins the “Most Promising African Artist” award at the 2004 Kora Music Awards

Bil Aka Kora’s “djongo music” fuses Kassena rhythms from the Gurunsi with modern arrangements that explicitly incorporate reggae and jazz influences, framed as original and sophisticated. Tim Winsé, initially a dancer in international troupes, turns to music to promote the Samo lolo musical bow, singing with modern instrumental accompaniment. Wuzzi (a group) win several awards at Semaine Nationale de la Culture; Sami Rama, a Bissa singer born in Côte d’Ivoire, pursues an international career after winning the 2002 Kundé d’Or Best Singer award. Amadou Balaké enhances his international profile and represents Burkina Faso within pan-African salsa by joining Africando All Stars in 2000.

Urban dance preferences in Ouagadougou move through successive imported styles: Congolese ndombolo (from Congo), followed by Ivorian mapouka, and then coupé-décalé, which spawns a synthesizer-based style and a rapid succession of DJ-produced hits that gradually displace live bands in the maquis (open-air bar-restaurants).
