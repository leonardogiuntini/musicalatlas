Early JUJU is linked to the working-class identity of YORUBA city-dwellers and to audiences of recently urbanized Yoruba and non-Yoruba transients working in Lagos and other West African ports. Nigerian Juju’s roots lie in GUITAR, BANJO, and UKULELE music emerging in the 1920s, combined with the more traditional genre ***KONKOMA (chorus, mbira-type hand-piano, drums), linked to Ghanaian-derived PALM-WINE styles popular in Lagos in the 1930s–1940s and part of an urban West African palm-wine guitar tradition. Lagos neighborhood competition: “each quarter in Lagos had its favorite juju band” (1930s).

Nigeria’s record industry emerges in the early 1920s (marketing phonograms to emerging middle/upper classes); mid-1920s local recordings pressed as shellacs for sale in Nigeria. European commercial recording in/around Lagos beginning in 1928. Proto-highlife via colonial martial band → African dance-orchestra transition → Lagos-based Calabar Brass Band recording (1920s–30s) as the Lagos Mozart Orchestra.

--1968–1973, “beginning of military rule” and “end of the civil war”; oil rapidly replacing other foreign-revenue sources. Civil war (1967–1970) drives “many of the best Igbo musicians” to leave Lagos. Highlife dance bands are said to have gone out of style in Nigeria during the disruptive period of the civil war, marking a political moment that reshapes the country’s dance-band scene. Oil boom of the 1970s expanding patronage for neotraditional celebrations and increasing jùjú band number/size.

AFRO-BEAT begins (late 1960s) as confluence of dance-band highlife, jazz, and soul. It is tied to Fela Kuti’s bands and Tony Allen’s drumming in the 1970s heyday of Africa 70. Fela Kuti, explicitly framed as central to modern Nigerian music, uses his Afro-beat to denounce exploitative businesses and government corruption and oppression (“Music is a weapon”); his repeated imprisonments for criticizing the Nigerian government link popular music to political dissent. Fela turns his compound into a nightclub/scene and eventually the Kalakuta Republic, declaring it independent from the Federal Republic of Nigeria; military attack on “Kalakuta Republic” (1977), subsequent political hardening and Egypt 80 shift.

FUJI, rooted in Yoruba Islamic communities (succeeding Apala), emerging as genre label in the late 1960s, is described as extremely popular among Lagos lower classes, representing a new, percussion-only Nigerian genre that speaks directly to urban working-class life. Pioneered by Sikiru Ayinde Barrister, it derives from WERE and AJISARI wake-up singing tradition. Kollington Ayinla. Wasiu Ayinde Barrister.

WAKA rises among Yoruba concurrent with Fuji; adopted from Muslim HAUSA ceremonies—typically women performers, vocally oriented with Yoruba talking drums/hand percussion. Batile Alake, Kubura Alaragbo, Queen Salawa Abeni are named as prominent women who popularized wákà.

Among Hausa, Dan Maraya described as one of northern Nigeria’s greatest praise singers, performing primarily on Songhai/Hausa single string lute KONTIGI.

In the 1970s, juju becomes further modernized and commercialized under Ebenezer Obey and King Sunny Ade, both of whom develop international audiences; this marks Nigerian juju as part of a global pop economy. Older juju songs are often praise songs for patrons or public figures; since the early 1970s, social commentary becomes more common in the texts, and religious songs abound as many juju musicians are fervent Christians. General Prince Adekunle, Lady Balogun, Moses Olaiya.
