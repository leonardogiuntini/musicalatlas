Ghana is described as a British colony on Africa’s “Gold Coast”, situated within a West African region heavily affected by the Atlantic slave trade during the broader colonial period (early 1500s–late 1800s).

Colonial Gold Coast society with elite “high-class, top-hat-and-tails” dance evenings, where local ballroom and RAGTIME bands ***(Excelsior Orchestra, Jazz Kings of Accra, Accra Orchestra, Cape Coast Sugar Babies) entertained the Ghanaian elite in clubs and dance halls. After 1870, stationing of West Indian colonial soldiers in Cape Coast and Elmina and their Afro-Caribbean music inspired coastal Ghanaian musicians, portrayed as innovators who first refashion regimental, classical, theatrical and church musics, than spread theese through southern Ghana.

Early HIGHLIFE emerges from combining local idioms and rhythms like FANTE Osibisaaba and ADAHA brass-band music with KRU GUITAR styles from Liberia, ASIKO music from Sierra Leone, European church music, military brass-band music and sea shanties in the ports and coastal towns.

--- Early twentieth century --- The term “highlife” is coined for the fashionable elite dance evenings, marking a named urban dance culture under colonial rule. Kwame Asare (Jacob Sam), pioneer highlife guitarist who learned from a Kru sailor in the 1920s, and his Kumasi Trio, made the first highlife recordings in London in 1928. Since 1930s, recording industry connects Ghana to London (Zonophone, HMV, Parlophone), with Ghanaian musicians travelling to record, embedding Ghana in early transnational music circuits.

PALM-WINE music: early twentieth-century dockside and bar music combining local West African instruments with portable instruments of visiting seamen (CONCERTINA, HARMONICA, GUITAR). George William Aingo was one of the most famous early Fante palm-wine guitarists. As palm wine moves inland, it absorbs features of the AKAN SEPREWA (harp-lute), creating the more “rootsy” ODONSON or Akan “BLUES” style, recorded by early guitarists/vocalists like Appiah Adjekum, Kwame Mireku, Kwesi Pepera, Osei Bonsu, Kwesi Menu and Kamkan.

CONCERT-PARTY: local VAUDEVILLE / MUSIC-HALL format for elites with tap-dancing, ragtime and short comic sketches in English, drawing on American blackface performance. Fanti comedian Bob Johnson takes the concert party away from urban elites into the “rural hinterland”, starting its indigenization and linking it to Akan storytelling traditions and folklore.

KONKOMBA marching bands arise in 1930s Akan towns and villages that cannot afford brass instruments, using local drums and voices instead.

***Husunu Adonu Afadi-Ladzekpo – son of Kofi Zate and leader-poet/composer for the Yevavo Axatsewu Club, which formed in the late 1930s; his songs are preserved in Lasibi’s repertory, showing intergenerational transmission.

--- 1940s–50s --- During WWII, presence of thousands of British and American servicemen introduces SWING and JAZZ into Ghana’s urban music, providing audiences and influences for bands that later become highlife dance bands.

Servicemen Bob Vans fighting in Burma (Myanmar) run concert-parties for troops, then returned and found the group Burma Jokers and later the Ghana Trio, linking wartime experience to post-war popular theatre and nationalist politics.

After WWII, E.T. Mensah & His Tempos Band initiate a professional highlife dance-band movement: big-band urban dance music enriched with swing, jazz, AFRO-CUBAN percussion and CALYPSO. Guy Warren (Kofi Ghanaba), master drummer and early Tempos' member, introduced Afro-Cuban percussion into highlife and later moved to the US recording seminal Afro-jazz albums. King Bruce, jazz and swing loving trumpeter, founded multiple bands like Black Beats and Barbecues de Ghana.

Led by E.K. Nyame (key figure trained with Appiah Adjekum), guitar-band highlife evolves from palm-wine groups and expands using dance-band instruments. Comic highlife opera emerges when Nyame’s Akan Trio attaches a concert-party to his guitar-band, a model adopted widely by 1950s guitar-bands like I.E. Mason, Kwaa Mensah, Kwabena Nyama, Uhuru Dance Band, Kakaiku, Yamoah, T.O. "Jazz" Ampoumah, Jaguar Jokers, Bob Cole, Onyina’s Royal Trio and Dr. Gyasi’s Noble Kings, touring villages and performing acoustic and early electric highlife tied to popular theatre.

After independence in 1957, Kwame Nkrumah’s socialist-aligned government promotes indigenous music, funds professional performing-arts troupes and Workers Brigade concert-parties, and travels with full dance bands as part of his political entourage. Bob Ansah was a concert-party leader whose group supported the nationalist cause. Rising nationalist struggle, rapid urbanization and social change provide fertile ground for classic highlife of the 1950s–60s, that becomes Ghana’s “urban dance music”, with original songs in English and local languages, incorporating traditional rhythms into modern arrangements and sometimes venturing into Congo music and calypso.

--- 1960s–1970s --- the “Golden Era” of Ghanaian music, focusing largely on Accra, the capital. Ghanaian music absorbs Congolese music, POP and especially SOUL, whose “black and proud” ethos and fashions encourage experimentation.

Jerry Hansen – Leader of the Ramblers International Dance Band (formed 1962), whose fully professional fifteen-man line-up sustained a living from shows and records for nearly twenty years.

KPANLOGO drum-dance, created in early 1960s by Otoo Lincoln, Frank Lane and Okule Foes, becomes a key GA recreational rhythm later revived and internationally popular. Mustapha Tettey Addy also helped create kpanlogo and later formed the Obonu Drummers.

In early 1970s, Afro-rock, AFRO-BEAT and Afro-funk appear in Ghana with bands like Psychedelic Aliens, Big Beats, Boombaya, Sawaaba Soundz, African Brothers International Band and Osibisa (London based). Afro-roots fusion groups such as Hedzoleh Soundz, Basa Basa Soundz and the Bunzus Band dig into Ghanaian culture and present it in sophisticated fusion packages. Mac Tontoh (Osibisa) later formed Kete Warriors.

Koo Nimo – Folk guitarist who learned palm-wine techniques from Kwame Asare, Kwaa Mensah and E.K. Nyame, later adding CLASSICAL-GUITAR, European and American jazz influences; leads the acoustic Adadam Agoromma Group.

Nii Tei Ashitey and Wulomei, leaders of the Ga cultural revival, encourage youth “to forget foreign music and do their own thing”. Other Ga cultural troupes like Bukom, Dzadzeloi, Blemabii Band, Abladei Culture Group, Ashiedu Keteke and Suku Troupe promote “rootsy” Ga music.

Back-to-roots acoustic palm-wine returns as some guitar-band veterans (Kwaa Mensah, T.O. “Jazz” Ampoumah, Kwabena Nyama) move back from electric to acoustic formats.

DAGBAMBA drummer Abubakari Lunna works as a professional with the Ghana National Folkloric Company, retires from government service in 1988, then returns to northern Ghana to serve in the Dagbon courtly system until his father’s death. Lunna’s life story underlines the contrast between “the north” and “the south” of Ghana.

In late 70s, the Anya Agbekor Society illustrates urban working-class Ghanaian contexts for EWE music in Accra, with musicians like Godwin Agbeli, Evans Amenumey, Gideon Foli Alorwoyie and Dogbevi Abaglo.

The Gambian KORA player Foday Musa Suso met Adam Rudolph in Ghana; together they formed the Mandingo Griots Society, basically a rock band with the kora out front, showing Ghana as a meeting place for transnational, experimental ensembles.

-- From late 1970s, dance-band highlife declines amid new imported pop. Due to economical crisis, maintaining large full-time bands becomes “near impossible” as clubs and dance halls close and equipment breaks down. Many musicians emigrate to Europe and North America and, within Afro-pop “world music,” Ghana contributes the enduring highlife genre and joins Nigeria and South Africa in dominating African music exports: in Britain, highlife and Ghanaian forms through artists like Kwabena Oduro-Kwarteng, Kofi Adu, Herman Asafo-Agyei, Sam Ashley, Alfred “Kari” Bannerman, Jon Kay and Ben Brako, added African elements to the 1980s “world music” boom and feeded into bands such as Hi-Life International, Orchestre Jazira, Kabbala and Dade Krama; in Germany, highlife fused with funk and rock to produce a harder-edged studio BURGER-HIGHLIFE electronic sound, beginning with George Darko’s “Akoo Te Brafo” and continuing with Kantata, Daddy Lumba and Nana Acheampong, producing dancefloor-oriented disco/highlife hybrids popular at home; Pat Thomas, A.B. Crentsil (Ahenfo Band), Jewel Ackah and Herman Asafo-Agyei moved to or recorded in Canada and U.S.A.; other diaspora-linked musicians: Sloopy Mike Gyanfi, Obo Addy, Andy Vans, Nana Tsiboe, Afro Moses, Okyeame Asante, Bob Pinado, Eddie Omensah.

In Nigeria, during its oil boom, Ghanaian highlife bands like Okukuseku, The Canadoes, Opambuas, Odoywewu, Kuul Strangers, Golden Boys, Citystyle and Beach Scorpions (often one-off albums) flourish particularly in Lagos and IGBO regions.

At home, by the early 1980s only two studios survive and cassette piracy spreads, prompting musicians to organize and lobby for government copyright protection and anti-piracy laws. Field recordings such as the Odo ye few korye kuw Vocal Band in the Fante area (Abura Tuakwa, Ghana, 1984) document village-based ensembles.

GOSPEL-HIGHLIFE: numerous Pentecostal/evangelical groups like Genesis Gospel Singers and Christo Asafo Mission, and female stars like Helen Rhabbles, Mary Ghansah, Diana Akiwumi, Tagoe Sisters and Daughters of Glorious Jesus play variants of highlife, making gospel a dominant commercial style, with around 60% of commercial pop output by the 1990s and with many churches running their own studios.

REGGAE and reggae-highlife gain strong followings with Kojo Antwi’s soft reggae in TWI, K.K. Kabobo’s reggae highlife, Rocky Dawuni, and especially Amekye Dede’s reggae-highlife idiom.

Filmmaker and musician Ahmed Adam popularizes the INDIAN-STYLE/LINE, a music sub-genre in Tamale heavily influenced by BOLLYWOOD musicals; in the mid-1990s, some rhythms and melodies are direct replicas of particular Bollywood songs, with Dagbanli lyrics substituted; Memunatu Lardi, Alfa Tuferu and Sherifatu Issah evolve beyond direct covers to create original melodies and rhythms.

Continued classical highlife output ranges from acoustic palm-wine to electric guitar bands with groups and artists like African Heroes, Amekye Dede, Alex Konadu, Alpha Waves, Gold Coasters, Western Diamonds, J.A. Adofo’s City Boys, Teacher and his Afrikana, Ebo Taylor, Gyedu Blay-Ambolley, Nana Tuffour, Felix Owusu, Megastar Band, Nakorex and it's artits solo projects Nat “Amandzeba” Brew, Akosua Agyepong and Rex Omar. The Smithsonian Folkways release “Rhythms of Life, Songs of Wisdom: Akan Music from Ghana, West Africa (1996)” presents Akan musics, including talking drums and vocal bands, to global audiences. New roots-fusion musicians and ensembles like Afrikan Show Boys, Atongo Zimba, Aaron Bebe Sukura, King Ayisoba, Hewale Sounds, Pan African Orchestra, Agorsor Cultural Ensemble, Takashi, Wassa Afrika, Selorm Cultural Troupe, Osei Korankye, “Oscarmore” Ofori, Stan Plange, Atongo Zimba and Captain Yaba blend traditional rhythms and instruments like ATENTEBEN flute and northern Ghanaian XALAM (MOLO or KOLIKO) lutes with contemporary song forms.

-- Mid-1990s: HIP-LIFE (hip-hop highlife) emerges as an urban youth genre, with lyrics in Twi and Ga and imagery drawn from U.S.A. RAP culture, giving city youth an “artistic voice”, with musicans like Reggie Rockstone, Nana King, Daasebre, Lord Kenya, Akatakyie, Nana Quame, Native Funk Lords, Tic Tac, Ex Doe & Chicago, Terry Bonchaka, Buk-Bak, Castro, Adane Best, Omanhene Pozo, Obour, Okonfo Kwade and Sydney.

Southern Ghana’s Akan culture dominates Ghana’s popular culture, especially in hiplife, highlife and hip-hop, whose lyrics focus heavily on Akan language and culture, while media from marginalized northern ethnic groups is relegated to the periphery. Despite marginalization, Northern Ghanaian artists produce music addressing social, political, economic and cultural themes for local audiences, blending Dagbanli language and Dagbamba cultural elements (proverbs, wise sayings, rhythmic and melodic patterns, values and analogies) with “Afro-American and Western-inspired idioms,” giving rise to what observers call a self-authenticated “Hybrid Pop Identity”. Prince Mahama, an early contemporary pop instrumentalist and blind musician from Tamale, teaches guitar to younger artists, including Sheriff Ghale. Hiplife artists in Tamale like Lil-K, Deensi, Don Sigli, Fancy Gadam, Maccasio and Lord Destro produce mixtapes by downloading popular instrumentals from the internet and rewriting them with their own lyrics, often in Dagbanli, integrating global beats into local narratives (global pop via internet-enabled remix culture). Kassim Gazor, described as one of the pioneering contemporary music artists in Tamale, is a non-literate “musical griot” who composes and “writes” songs in his mind, records ideas on his mobile phone, and later develops them in the studio, demonstrating a complex oral–digital creative process. Other artists from Northern Ghana: Abu Sadiq, Prince Okla, “7 in 1 for Peace” Collective, Optional King.

In recent years, a Kpanlogo derived dance called AZONTO becomes widely popular across Ghana, spreads outside the country and appears in Afro-pop music videos.

***Neotraditional drum dances like Ga kpanlogo and Ewe borborbor are taught alongside older traditions, indicating a continuing process of innovation within “tradition”.
