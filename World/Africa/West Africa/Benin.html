Benin’s position astride savanna (north) and forest (south) means that its musical life continually reflects both savanna political traditions (empires, Islamic courts, professional praise specialists) and forest traditions (fragmented polities, masquerades, secret societies, and complex rhythmic ensembles). In the “Benin Gap” (covering roughly present-day Benin, Togo, and SE Ghana), the savanna cuts through the forest to the Atlantic, and rivers and lagoons facilitate movement and cultural interchange between forest and savanna societies. Studies on societies in southern Benin (as part of the Eastern Forest cluster) show that this region exemplifies forest-belt patterns: complex percussion-based ensembles, elaborate court music and masquerades, and secret societies and age-grades as key institutions, with some adoption of Islam and Christianity.- 1600-1904: Dahomey Kingdom (FON People).

- 1960-1972- first years of independent Dahomey, with a quite vibrant music scene. Urban nightlife thrives through dance-band styles strongly influenced by Ghanaian and Nigerian HIGHLIFE, AFRO-CUBAN music, CONGOLESE-RUMBA, American SOUL and French CHANSON. An Afro-Cuban direction is paved by El Rego et ses Commandos and further developed by Gnonnas Pedro y sus Panchos. City bands such as Picoby Band d'Abomey, Super Star de Ouidah and Anassua Jazz de Parakou embody these Afro-Cuban / highlife-inflected nightclub styles.

Benin is described as having a strong base in Vodoun / Voodoo rhythms, which are used and re-interpreted by groups and artists both inside Benin and in the diaspora across the eras, including Le Roi Alokpon, Amikpon and Gbétchéou. HONGAN and KAKAGBO percussion ritually used by the GUN “Zangbeto”, Vodoun guardians of the night, later reworked into modern patterns by Sagbohan Danialou.

- 1972–1990- Kérékou’s dictatorship, explicitly described as a “Marxist-Leninist” regime, renaming Dahomey as Benin. Artists are expected to praise those in power; a strict curfew leads to the end of “wild nightlife” and the scene at home “grinds to a halt.” Many musicians move abroad (e.g., settling in Paris and elsewhere), partly as a response to political pressure. Under the regime, a push toward “seeking their own traditions” leads to hybrid tradi-modern styles (as documented on the Sonorama Sud Bénin CD/DVD): TCHINKOUME, rooted in funeral contexts, becomes a creative source for new electric/percussion-heavy dance music like TCHINK-SYSTEM, created by Tohon Stan. ZINLI (Traditional royal court music), originally restricted to royal funerals at the Abomey king’s court, later becomes the basis for modern works by artists like Alèkpéhanhou. Urban Groove of T.P. Orchestre Poly-Rythmo mixes Afro-soul, AFRO-BEAT, SOUKOUS, Cuban sounds and FUNK. Nel Oliver absorbs a range of Afro-American influences to create this powerful hybrid AFRO-AKPALA-FUNK style. Mainstream 1970s African pop Cella Stella, stylistically aligned with the broader African pop of that era.

-1990s/early 2000s- About fifteen years of democracy, even if Kéréku is legally elected again in 1996, are credited with strengthening an enthusiastic new tradi-modern sound. Modernized fishermen’s AGBADJA from the southwest becomes very popular through artists like Gbessi-Zolawadji. Gangbe Brass Band mixes big-band JAZZ sounds with traditional percussion patterns, Benin “voudoun music”, influences from neighbouring countries, echoes of African military bands and European brass bands. Denagan Janvier Honfo’s voodoo-based percussion music blends traditional percussion with vocal polyphonies and later with European brasses. Angélique Kidjo’s cross-genre work combines soul, R&B, funk-rock, Caribbean styles, Brazilian, Cuban and other genres with Beninois rhythms like Gogbahoun and Fon lyrics. REGGAE – Yaya Yaovi launches a reggae group after visiting Bob Marley’s Tuff Gong studio. RAP and Afro-rap with French lyrics. Ardiess Posse and H2O innovate by rapping in Fon and adapt voodoo rhythms to socially engaged rap. K-Sim’s fusion draws on soul, JAZZ and HIP-HOP and uses traditional instruments and rhythms from Sahel cultures (mostly FULANI).

Other musicians: Ignacio Blazio Osho & Orchestra Las Ondas, G.G. Vikey, L'Orchestre Black Santiago – founded in 1966 by Ignace de Souza, Honoré Avolonto, Orchestre Les Volcans Du Benin, Ambroise Akoha, Bluecky d’Almeida, Vivi l’Internationale, John Arcadius, Bobo We, Vano Baby.
