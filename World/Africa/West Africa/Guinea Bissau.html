More than three centuries of Portuguese domination leave almost no formal musical heritage beyond backyard and ceremonial music. Jaliyaa is framed as a “classical” tradition of the Mande.

In the early 1930s, ethnomusicologist George Herzog undertakes one of the first detailed studies of performance in the Guinea Coast area.

'backyard beats' centre on Gumbay and related KRIOL styles (tina, tinga) as well as ethnic forms such as KUSSUNDE and brosca/broxa (BALANTA), djambadon (Mandinga) and kundere (Bijagos), all closely tied to funerals, spirit-calling, initiation and harvest rituals.

A long liberation struggle culminates in independence in the mid-1970s; Kriolu music becomes central to mobilizing and uniting the population. José Carlos Schwarz's and Aliu Bari's Le Cobiana Djazz's Kriolu music is taken up as mother of all contemporary GB bands, defining GUMBE as a new national genre as a mixture of traditional musics, helping defining indipendence. In 1973 Ernesto Dabό (later Djôrçon) records "M’Ba Bolama", a gumbe-style Kriolu song and first modern Guinea Bissau single. Super Mama Djombo's music mixes gumbe with more sophisticated band arrangements and electric guitar.

The 1980s are marked by deep economic crisis: shops are almost empty and there is a chronic lack of food; songs move from revolutionary optimism toward social and political critique, and some singers are jailed or banned. Socially engaged solo records by José Carlos Schwarz, Sidonio Quaresma and Ramiro Naka retain narrative lyricism while adapting to faster disco-type dance grooves; Zé Manel, drummer with Super Mama Djombo records the album 'Tustumunhus di Aonti' (1983) with strong political lyrics and is banned from public performance. Justino Delgado becomes popular after being jailed for sarcastic songs about President Nino Vieira. Salvador Embaló Tchando spends two years in jail before emigrating. Kaba Mané’s work is described as a modern kussundé style with kora-like electric guitar.

In the 1990s, Guinea-Bissau enters a phase described as 'Survival and War': IMF-backed liberalization yields little improvement, even as clubs and discos multiply in Bissau; bands such as Tabanka Djaz and Maio Coope & Gumbezarte achieve the first significant commercial breakthroughs, offering an exciting Kriolu mix that incorporates various ethnic styles while remaining constrained by limited recording resources, while Manecas Costa’s work foregrounds gumbe rhythms in refined acoustic settings that address hunger, corruption and suffering.

A civil war in 1998–99 devastates the city, destroying venues and bands.

In post-war Guinea-Bissau, many bands have disintegrated and numerous musicians live in exile; gumbe remains the rhythmic backbone of club and radio playlists, even as other African dance genres, Stevie Wonder-style soul and salsa share the space, with artists like Guto Pires and Nino Galissa. Bidinte blends West African, Euro-Arabian and flamenco elements with Kriolu lyrics.
