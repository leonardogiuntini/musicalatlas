West Africa is portrayed as the part of Africa where polyrhythmic, multilayered music is especially evident, shaped by a heritage of cultural interchange with North African and Arab traditions, particularly in savannas and deserts.

Across West Africa, the figure of the professional or semiprofessional musician tends to occupy a distinct and often hereditary social niche, mediating between rulers, ancestors, spirits and communities through historical song, praise and ritual performance, who were vital parts of state occasions.

Traditional structures, religious practices, hereditary court-based musician roles and dense networks of dance clubs remain central features of social and ceremonial life, indicating only moderate adjustment and continuity of precolonial practices into the contemporary era.

In the savanna region, there is much uniformity in musical culture, particularly in social organization, musician roles and status, types of instruments and styles of performance, although differences remain between regional clusters (Western Sudanic, Central Sudanic, Voltaic).

Because of ongoing contacts, Central Sudanic music is relatively uniform: North-African influence is most evident in ceremonial music and instrument types; Hausa music dominates the cluster, influencing central and southwest Nigeria, the Guinea coast and Voltaic peoples. Courtly ensemble performance for state occasions is treated as part of a general pattern shared by ancient West African empires, whose wealth from long-distance trade allowed to maintain large ensembles of royal musicians. Their performances acted as sonic symbols that paralleled visual symbols of power such as cloth and gold-covered swords, stools and staves.

Forest-belt music is characterized by complex rhythm, extensive use of percussive instruments, and elaborated court and masquerade traditions. Musicians provide soundtracks for life-cycle events, agricultural work, festivals, religious rites and political. The Eastern Forest cluster, used as a model for West African forest music, is characterized by smaller-scale nation-states or confederacies, age-grade systems and secret societies, with external influences from savanna dwellers and Europeans.

Across West Africa, praise-singing griots or jeli are highlighted as a key professional musician type; they often come from families of musicians who train their children to carry on the occupation, embodying a hereditary model of musical specialization within the region. In each Western Sudanic ethnic group, professional or full-time musicians bear specific titles; for example, dyeli among the Manding of Mali, jali among Maninka of Guinea and Mandinka of The Gambia, gewel among Wolof, and gawlo among Fulɓe.

The Central Sudanic cluster (Songhai, Djerma, Dendi, Hausa, Fulani, Kanuri, Jukun, Tiv) mostly live in Nigeria, Niger, Mali and Burkina Faso; groups in the Central Sudanic cluster did not experience a major dismantling of their traditional social structure as a result of colonial policies. Consequently, patronage and social stratification remain more intact than in the Western Sudanic cluster.

In Central Sudanic cultures (HAUSA, FULANI, DAGBAMBA), musicians are largely full-time specialists and urbanites belonging to a distinct social class. Ceremonial musicians linked to traditional power hold high status, while others enjoy lower but still recognized status. Musical styles are strongly text-driven: melody and rhythm track the tones and quantities of tonal languages, and instruments often imitate speech; solo or small-ensemble singing with dense melismatic ornamentation is common.

Historically, West Africa includes centralized kingdoms (such as Mali), “stateless societies” with less hierarchical chief-based structures and nomadic peoples that coexist alongside modern national governments.

- West Africa’s savanna zone, is the setting for major empires (Ghana to about 1200, Mali 1200s–1400s), influencing trade and population movements.

- After about 1600, the history of the Sudanic empires is described as one of fracture and gradual decline, affected by internal rebellions.
