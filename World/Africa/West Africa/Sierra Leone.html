Most speakers of West Atlantic languages—Temne, Sherbro, Limba, Gola, Kissi—live in Sierra Leone. Temne history is sketched as migration from the mountainous region of Jallonkadu (later part of Futa Djallon) to the coast north of the Sherbro.There is considerable variety of music even within each group, and it would be futile to try and cut up Sierra Leone music into tribal sections.

Freetown is shaped by Caribbean freed slave immigrants, who give the capital its name and an enduring KRIO culture, creating a cosmopolitan, coastal urban setting for later musical development. KRU sailors from Liberia work on foreign ships, including early Portuguese vessels that bring guitars, which they adopt and spread along the West African coast. Early moral panic around GUMBAY (african musical style repatriated from jamaican MAROON) is documented in 1858, when the African and Sierra Leone Weekly Advertiser journal denounces 'Gumbay dancing' as a cause of vices.

Gumbay is played by Ebenezer Calendar, whose "African Elegant - Sierra Leone's Kru / Krio Calypso Connection" recordings document a coastal Kru and Freetown Krio musical life, tiying to CALYPSO song forms and leanding to MARINGA, local name for PALM-WINE music.

Sierra Leone gains independence from Britain in 1961. From then onward, it suffers gross mismanagement, coups d’état, neo-colonial pressures and a venal, entrenched elite, undermining what should be a prosperous, resource-rich country.

Ebenezer Calendar is a mainstay of the Sierra Leone Broadcasting Service, whose recordings made for radio in the late 1950s/early 1960s capture both his independence-era songs and traditional material from communities all over the country. S.E. Rogie develops an "effortlessly laidback" palmwine guitar style.

HIGHLIFE, after its decline in popularity since the mid-1960s, contributed to the evolution of several regional forms that continue to be popular, like the stronger rock and disco orientation MILO-JAZZ, popularized by Dr. Oloh and his Milo-Jazz Band.

The 1970s are explicitly named as Sierra Leone’s musical heyday, when bands that grew up in the years after independence develop their own styles and sing about "sweet Salone" (krio for Sierra Leone), "swinging Freetown" and romantic/sexual themes. Their often explicit lyrics bring some bands into conflict with the post-independence authorities. Groups such as Afro National, Muyei Power, Sabanoh 75 and Super Combo Kings forge a style dubbed SOCA-BEAT. Geraldo Pino crafts a heavy funky big-band sound, influencing development of AFRO-BEAT and Afro-Pop. Bunny Mack and Abou Whyte.

Even as creativity peaks, the national recording industry "dies" in the 1970s.

In the 1980s and 1990s, the few notable success stories are largely those of exiled musicians recording and performing abroad.    Eleven years of civil war (1991–2002) further devastate the nation; war and economic hardship contribute to the disappearance of certain local styles and limit the possibilities for cultural life. U.S.A. based Daddy Ramanu uses a combination of "traditional" and calypso/highlife influences to create dancefloor-oriented beats. King Masco’s 1990s output consists of palmwine-based, ribald songs aimed at Sierra Leonean audiences at home and abroad. Abdul Tee-Jay, veteran London-based multi-instrumentalist. German based Ansumana Bangura howcases his "fankadama" approach, combining highlife, palmwine, traditional society music and milo-jazz into uptempo, percussive tracks. Great Steady Bongo maintains a presence within Sierra Leone. Ngoh Gbetuwai, Mende musician. Seydu, Freetown-born multi-instrumentalist influenced by his Fulani-Mandinka mother’s chanting.

In the late 1990s, returning exile Jimmy B establishes the Paradise studio in Freetown, which becomes a launch pad for a new generation of artists like Sisters With Attitude and Janka Nabay. Postwar youth frustration with corrupt leaders is encapsulated in Daddy Saj’s and Wan Pot Sojas' hits. A large gospel industry, with artists like Vicky Fornah and Johnny Wisdom, prospers among listeners primarily interested in religious messages rather than musical innovation.
