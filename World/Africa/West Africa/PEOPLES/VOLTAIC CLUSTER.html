The MOSSI / BARIBA are distinguished by their location in the Benin Gap, an area covering roughly present-day Benin, Togo, and southeast Ghana, where the savanna breaks through the forest to the Atlantic; rivers and lagoons there foster intense movement and cultural interchange between forest and savanna populations.Mossi kingdoms rise to power in the 1400s–1500s and fight the Songhai over control of the Niger Bend, while pushing southward against Gourmantché and Kusasi—peoples within the Voltaic cluster dispersed in Burkina. • LoDagaa-related societies (including Lobi, Gan, Gouin, Wara etc.) occupy northwest Ghana and adjacent parts of Burkina Faso, already sharing Voltaic cultural traits and funeral xylophone traditions that tie these frontier zones into a common cultural area.
Bariba occupy Borgu (northwest of Yorubaland) in the 1500s; their kingdom later reaches its height in the 1700s. Growing acceptance of Islam in Mossi kingdoms opens communication between MossiBariba Voltaic groups and Western/Central Sudanic clusters, affecting Bariba communities.

Larle Naaba, traditional head of all Mossi griot musicians, maintains his function vis-à-vis Mossi kings as genealogist, counsellor, historian and musician; he leads his own troupe and teaches musicians in his own royal court, exemplifying hereditary court musician roles.

Professional musicians form a distinct social class and dominate musical life. They attach themselves to royalty or important persons, enjoy high social status and function as historians, genealogists, and moral commentators through their praise songs.

Vocal and instrumental music is characterized by high tessitura, predominantly pentatonic scale systems, tense vocal quality, and modest ornamentation. Islamic celebrations frequently integrate traditional    music, especially historical or genealogy songs performed by drummers and fiddlers at Islamic events, underlining a syncretic religious-musical style.

SENUFO communities maintain Poro and Sande (brang by MANE, mande XVIc invaders) initiation-society music: water-drum and xylophone-based ensembles at “coming-out” and funerals, with pentatonic scales, instrumental polyphony (especially xylophones), and monodic vocal music. Among Senufo communities, there is a caste system for some occupations but no separate musician caste; instead, music is integrated into Poro and other social structures, with specialists embedded in ritual roles rather than a separate hereditary musician class.

DOGON communities maintain strong continuity of identity, customs, and religion, with limited outside influence on their musical culture. emphasize bullroarer and drum-centered music, masked dances, and collective dancing.

Burkina Faso; Mali

In KASENA music, royal and state occasions (durbars, festivals, events honoring dignitaries) become important musical contexts cultivated during colonial rule, indicating how praise songs and ensemble pieces come to represent traditional authority and develop within new colonial structures.

Musicians are semiprofessional, they farm or work in other jobs and play when needed. They hold high social status as respected community members rather than as a marginalized caste.

Royal/state music among Kasena becomes more prominent in colonial rule, with aerophone–drum ensembles playing heptatonic, polyphonic music (third-based harmony resolving to unison).

Benin(?), Burkina Faso; Ghana

DAGBAMBA do not have a single word equivalent to the Western idea of “music,” since music, dance and drama are closely bound.

Dagbon emerges as a hierarchical, centralized kingdom of the Dagbamba in northern Ghana; lunsi drummers are integral to this state structure.

Lunsi (griots) in Dagbaŋ are identified as custodians of culture who transmit histories from generation to generation, occupying a central socio-musical role within Northern Ghanaian society. Royal musicians attached to ancient kingdoms and to Dagbaŋ royal courts enhance state occasions and provide musical commentary on event

Through south-Saharan trade routes and ethnic migrations, and via Wangara and Hausa clerics, Islamic influences reach the north of contemporary Ghana; Naa Zanjina (1648–1677) adopts Islam as state religion in Dagbaŋ.

Ghana; Togo

In DAGAABA subcluster areas, long histories of migration and warfare make clans vulnerable to slave raiding until British colonial government in Ghana implants chieftainships. Historically, the origins of Dagaaba people has been a complex mystery to tackle, probably related to a schism with Dagbamba.

In LoDagaa areas, the local rise of Islam in the late twentieth century alters religious and musical life, contributing to a decline in xylophone-based practices associated with animism.
