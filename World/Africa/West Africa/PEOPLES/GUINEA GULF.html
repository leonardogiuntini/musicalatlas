EDO

Together with other ancient empires (Ancient Ghana, Mali, Songhai, Ashanti), Great Benin of Edo people flourished in the part of the present-day Nigeria from 1200s to 1800s, and participated in long-distance trade of gold, ivory and salt, accumulating wealth through this trade.

Musicians of Benin Kingdom have a presence as cultural representations through the museum display of bronze plaques showing horn players and drummers.

EWE

The GBE groups, including FON, GUN, and the EWE of Togo and modern Ghana, trace their common origin to Tado in modern Togo, on the left bank of the river Mono. In the 1500s, disputes within Tado lead to migrations: one section moves west to found Notsie, which becomes the center for Ewe dispersal over the region between the Mono and Volta rivers in Togo and Ghana. The Ewe who move west from this Togo-based center live precariously as a minority within powerful kingdoms of neighboring peoples and evolve more than 120 microstates differing in dialect and cultural traits, while related kin to the east create larger, more centralized units.

Ewe exodus in the late 1600s from the tyrannical king Agokoli of Nots, using fierce drumming to intimidate his warriors during escape. Migrating southwest, Ewe groups found settlements along a large lagoon near the mouth of the Volta River; the group led by Wenya, too tired to go farther, becomes known as the ANLO (cramped). Other Ewe families settle along the Atlantic coast and upland hills of this region, laying the basis for later communities in what became Ghana and Togo.

Hunters, regarded as repositories of knowledge given by God, possessing special herbs and the ability to communicate safely with dangerous animals, conduct traditional customs involving drumming, double bell, and worship, and in legends they are the ones who first bring the forest/monkey dance and drumming that becomes Agbekor (traditional war dance/drumming) back to the community.

The 18th and 19th centuries see frequent military conflicts with neighboring groups, with European traders, and among Ewe themselves; the Anlo-Ewe gain a fearsome reputation as warriors, forming the backdrop for war drumming traditions. Britain, Germany, and France administer Ewe territory during a brief colonial period from the 1880s to the 1950s, after which Ewe people live in the independent nation-states of Ghana and Togo. Christian missionaries whip students for attending traditional performance events, discouraging participation in Ewe music and dance. After Ghanas statehood in 1957, national government sponsors professional troupes in which Agbekor is a staple, contributing to broader Ewe cultural visibility.

Contemporary Ewe traditional music idioms with freshly composed songs, contrasted explicitly with Agbekor as an old repertory inherited from ancestors. Much of Ewe life focuses on dance clubs that function as entertainment, recreation, and ceremonial institutions; Each Ewe dance club has composers who create music in three named genres: axatsevu, akpewu, and specific style pieces characterized by distinctive drumming and dancing.

Funeral music in Anlo communities – dance-club repertories (e.g., Adzomani) performed at funerals; “good music” at funerals is described as the deepest expression of loss, and a funeral without music is lamented as “(what) a sad thing.”

IGBO

In Igbo and other Eastern Forest societies, music is used widely—at royal honors, assemblies, funerals, festivals and storytelling—and often involves masked performance and satirical topical songs; In Igbo society, music making is not a class or profession: individuals specialize according to talent and act as interpreters of the community’s music; training is informal, mainly by imitation, reflecting decentralized, non-urban political organization. Musical traits show influences from Central Africa: equal prominence of membranophones (igba) and slit-drums (ekwe, ufie), rich percussive instruments (drums, bells, rattles, percussion vessels, wooden clappers), and melodic instruments (xylophones, lamellophones, flutes, trumpets, musical bows, pluriarcs).

YORUBA people described as (predominantly) in SW Nigeria and partly in Kwara/Kogi (Nigeria), representing the Yoruba-Edo-Nupe subcluster.

Ile-Ife is the religious center, and people believe in common descent from Oduduwa. The political system is based on kingdoms ruled by divine kings, with the Ogboni secret society, a military organization and age-grades supporting royal power.

The YORUBA kingdom of Oyo, a major coastal state, rises before 1500 and declines in the early 1800s after civil wars; it maintains trade with Songhai, Hausa, and Nupe, linking Yoruba communities into wider northsouth networks. In Benin they maintain indigenous culture, especially religion, more fully than in Nigeria. Among the Yoruba (living mostly in southwest Nigeria), Islam has made inroads, but Christian missionary activity since the 1840s has checked its further advance, marking an important 19th-century religious shift.

Fulani conquest (1804–1808) under Usman Dan Fodio, incorporating the region into the Sokoto Caliphate. Dan Fodio’s campaign “profoundly shaped” Yoruba political geography.

Yoruba adopt waka from the Hausa “probably in the early 1800s.”

Genres and styles found among Yoruba include texts dominating praise chants, acclamations, and songs while instruments primarily accompany the voice.

“Drummunication” among South Western Yoruba people (Nigeria) described as an extra-mundane tool linking people with deities/spirits/ancestors, as communication between natural/supernatural worlds; extra-mundane channels include incantation, spiritual chants, prayers, invocations, séances, trances, panegyric praise, dirges sacrifices.

Among musicians, most are semiprofessional, relying on farming or weaving. Recruitment, especially of drummers, is patrilineal, and training is formal, usually through kin or established musicians. Most specialize either in particular instruments or in specific kinds of vocal music. Yoruba priests and religious specialists play central roles in music for orisa worship and oro ceremonies (Songs for oro, the secret society of night hunters that administers justice in Oron Nation), linking musical authority closely to ritual authority and associated with bullroarers and mirlitons.

Repertoires for Orisa worship, including possession and dance with chanted text. Yoruba voices often harmonize in fourths (rather than the thirds favored by some neighboring groups), giving vocal lines greater mobility.

there is a contrast between unison singing in the east and polyphony in the west, where harmonies in fourths allow greater mobility.
