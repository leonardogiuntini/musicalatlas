MANDE region is the centre of several great empires:

300s to 1200s - kingdom of Ghana;

1000s - Mandinka had organized a small state called Mali;

around 1235 - Mali Empire was founded by Sunjata Keita, unifying warring communities and arosing in the West African savanna between desert and forest, a cultural crossroads where Sudanic African, Tuareg, Berber, Arab and European civilizations met, connected by caravan routes between the Mediterranean and the Sudan and by rivers such as the Niger, Senegal and Gambia.

Within this imperial setting, JALIYAA (the professional art of jalolu) already played important roles in Mande civilization, serving the needs of elite patrons. Sunjata Keita and his personal jeli Bala Faseke Kouyaté (balafon player, captured by Sunjata enemy Susu Sumanguru because of his high value) anchor the origin myth of jeli lineages (all Kouyaté jelis are said to descend from Bala Faseke). Dyeli/jali form a hereditary caste of musicians, praise-singers, historians, royal counselors, educators, public entertainers and instrument-makers attached to aristocrats, warriors, and patrons, specializing in praise and historical songs with instruments like KORA and BALAFON. Long epic narratives, genealogies and praise songs about rulers such as Sunjata Keita and his generals form the core repertoire. Kings maintain courts where jelis entertain and educate nobility.

Jaliyaa music is framed as one of the world’s “classical” musics, defined partly by performance for elites and by a sophisticated, orally transmitted theoretical system, despite the absence of written notation or formal treatises.

1353 - Ibn Battuta visits Mali and describes Muslim feast days, Mansa Sulayman as “sultan”, Dugha as intermediary and master of royal musicians with lutes, drums, trumpets and bugles; this early Islamic context forms part of the historical heritage shared by Mande peoples.

Mali is described as fabulously wealthy and as a centralized, hierarchically organized empire with distinct social classes, Islamic libraries and universities in great cosmopolitan cities, and a class of literati (writing in Arabic) who managed commerce and law. Dyula people of Burkina Faso have their capital at Bobo-Dioulasso, a strategic commercial and cultural center whose strongest traditions are those of balafon and percussion.

Mande society is stratified: basic distinction between foro/foro (freeborn) and jongo (slaves); the ruler (mansa/sultan) and warriors are at the top of society and have music performed specifically for them; nobles (horon), artisan “castes” (nyamakala) including jelis, and descendants of slaves (jon).

Hunter societies (donsoton) function as inter-ethnic, egalitarian associations (excluding only women and children) whose members serve as guardians of villages, providers of meat and healers; their blues-like melodies and powerful rhythmic music is seen as among Africa’s oldest surviving musical forms and probably the ancestor of jeli music. Dance drumming and singing are identified as the non-classical counterpart to Jaliyaa: male drum troupes perform for village events, especially for women’s dancing, with women as the main subjects and participants of songs.

1400s - The Mali Empire went into decline, marking the end of Mali’s regional imperial dominance and followed by SONGHAI rule (1460–1591).

Mande diaspora begins, with lesser kingdoms remaining and trade networks kept intact. Jaliyaa follows regional power holders (war chiefs, merchants, later politicians) across the Mande world, spreading its culture, together with Islam, across West Africa.

1700s - French writings coin ‘griot’ as the term for hereditary professional musicians. Possible Arabic root of the term: some scholars suggest ‘griot’ may derive from Arabic ‘qawal’ (“word, speak, sound”), linking Dyula-related terminology partly to Arab linguistic influence. European travellers of the 18th–19th centuries see leaders with jali at their side, or jalolu improvising praise songs for nobles in streets; this practice is labelled “begging” in English but is in fact a cultural obligation of jaliyaa.

During the colonial period, traditional social structure broke down; both the role of the professional musician and the patronage system changed. Musicians no longer depended on the patronage of specific individuals, had to hunt for patrons, and begging became more common.

This social structure and the patron–jeli relationship survive into the 21st century, with presidents among those receiving countless praise songs. Standard jeli pieces – Songs like “Lamban”, “Sunjata”, “Turamaghan” and “Bambougoudji” are centuries-old but treated as improvisatory “blueprints”, constantly updated like jazz standards at weddings. Core jali families (Kouyaté, Suso/Sissoko, Jebateh/Jobarteh/Diabaté) dominate musical professions across the Mande world.

Les Ballets Africains structures Mande drumming and dancing into a theatrical ballet-style show for international audiences, a staging model that spreads to national troupes in all Mande-inhabited countries.

JAZZ genealogies projected onto jaliaa: foreign scholars and listeners sometimes interpret Mande styles as ancestral to jazz.

In recent years, Islamic extremist groups have terrorised musicians in Mali, compared explicitly to the Taliban’s suppression of music in Afghanistan.

KPELLE migrations into the area known as Liberia occurred between the 1400s and the 1800s, bringing Kpelle musical and social institutions into Liberian territory. Poro and Sande secret societies are established as vehicles for transmitting culture. Woi-meni-pele epic genre. Kpelle timekeepers concepts of time (months named after sound qualities; “year” as a “fence” enclosing social action) are linked to how performances are structured. proverb “if you build a town and there’s no drummer in it, then it’s not a town”.

Guinea Conakry; Liberia

DAN (linking Ivorian forest society to wider Mande networks), who straddle the borders of Côte d’Ivoire and Liberia, distinguish three main types of music: tan (‘dance song’, also ‘instrumental music’ and ‘dance’), zlee (‘praise song’), and gbo (‘funeral lament’).

Music-making is a highly regarded profession. Ge (mask institutions) are central religious and political authorities; chiefs’ power is legitimated through association with powerful village ge, with masks appearing at harvest celebrations and life-cycle rites like weddings and funerals. French colonial administrators mount large mask festivals.
