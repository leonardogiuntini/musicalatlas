FULƁE

(FULA/FULANI/PEUL), who are in the minority throughout the savanna from Senegal to Cameroon, originating in the middle Senegal area and formed through Tukulor–Berber intermixture, spread eastward and southward between the 1100s and the 1800s, settling among Soninke, Mandinka, Bambara, Hausa, and Mossi.

From the 1100s, Fulani spread across the savanna and later wage jihads to gain political dominance, reshaping Central Sudanic musical-political culture. Futa Jallon jihad (Guinea, 1725) launches the first West African jihad. Early in the 1800s, the Fulani conquered the Hausa and organized their territory into emirates.

Among muslim FULANI, professional drummers specialize in childrens dance songs and in castigation contests (soro/sharo), where they sing praises of young men and build morale and tension. For Fulani court musicians, songs praising chiefs and wealthy patrons, referring to genealogies and heroic exploits, form a central genre; some are attached to single patrons while others travel between courts. secular adaptations of previously religious poems also appear.

Around 1800, the genre of Fulfulde sung poems GIME arises and becomes part of the Central Sudanic Islamic literary-musical tradition. sung on religious themes and secular topics, in private or small gatherings and on special religious occasions, becomes a specialty of blind beggars as well as other performers. early examples come from Futa Djallon and Sokoto, but they represent a broader Fulani tradition within the Central Sudanic area.

Fulani herdsmen also perform a range of song types—work songs, lullabies, love songs, cattle-praise songs, children’s dance songs and dance songs (ruume, yake, geerewol) for youth.

HAUSA: founded major city-states including Kano (AD 993), Zaria (AD 1000), Katsina (AD 1100). The Hausa (a Central Sudanic people for whom Hausa is the mother tongue) at one point lived within the Songhai Empire, while also paying tribute to the king of Bornu.

Bori, identified later as a pre-Islamic religion among the Hausa that makes much use of music and dance, has roots in this pre- and early-Islamic era, even though it continues beyond it.

In the pre-emirate Hausa area, the spread of Islam (from Mali in the 1300s and via Kanuri) creates a contrast between Islamic ritual—where music is largely absent except for the call to prayer—and older practices such as bori, a pre-Islamic spirit-possession religion that makes much use of music and dance.

Islamization of the Hausa area begins well before 1800: many scholars believe Islam entered from Mali in the 1300s, possibly earlier via the Kanuri of the Bornu Empire; for a time the Hausa lived under Songhai rule and also paid tribute to the king of Bornu.

Hausa music includes forms later labelled state ceremonial music (rokon fada), court praise (yabon sarakai), and rural folk/popular music; these genres emerge in continuity with earlier political contexts under Songhai and Bornu influence and are framed by courtly structures that predate the Fulɓe conquest.

In the early 1800s, the Fulani (Sokoto Caliphate) conquest of the Hausa reorganized their territory into emirates; Hausa associated with Fulani “after the Fulani Jihad of the early 1800s”; Nineteenth-century Hausa practice within the emirate system gives rise to state ceremonial music (rokon fada), court praise (yabon sarakai) and rural folk/popular music.

In Hausa practice, apart from bori, music and dance have no direct association with religion; music other than the call to prayer is rarely heard in mosques or during Islamic ritual, even though Islamic holidays and political and religious life frame much musical activity. Bori remains an enduring genre: a pre-Islamic spirit-possession religion whose ceremonies use specific music to communicate with spirits; musicians for bori form a distinct professional category in Hausa culture.

In Hausa practice (aside from bori), music and dance have no direct association with religion; music (other than the call to prayer) is rarely heard in mosques or during Islamic ritual, even though Islamic holidays and political/religious life frame much musical activity. Bori, a pre-Islamic spirit-possession religion, makes much use of music and dance and is an enduring religious genre within the Central Sudanic zone.

Scholars distinguish several types of Hausa professional musicians: (1) musicians of occupational classes, (2) musicians in political life, (3) musicians of recreational music, (4) musician-entertainers, and (5) musicians for bori spirit possession.

Two types of praise songs are distinguished in the emirate context: urban classical praise (performed by professionals attached to a single patron, with relatively fixed stylistic and textual traits) and popular praise songs (performed by freelance musicians with many patrons, of more recent origin). Popular music rivals court praise in audience and artistry; instruments such as kalangu, goge, kukuma and kuntigi distinguish popular ensembles from court ensembles.

SONGHAI

began to dominate large parts of Sudanic Africa during the 1200s, reached its apogee in the early 1500s, extending from near the Atlantic in the west to include most Hausa states in the east and felt North African influence. its political and musical influence underpins later Hausa/Fulani.

In the 1400s and 1500s, as Mossi kingdoms rose to power, Mossi armies repeatedly fought the Songhai for control of the Niger Bend, connecting Voltaic Mossi polities with Songhai power centred along the Niger

Songhai music is divided into secular and religious (follay) categories. Secular music includes solo, choral (may be unaccompanied or accompanied by the KONTIGI lute) and instrumental pieces for wrestling matches and dances, work songs by men and women, and songs by children and adolescents in games and courtship; texts treat history, politics, legends, fables, satire and praise. Follay is religious music performed for a hierarchy of divinities associated with natural forces (sky, rain, thunder, earth, river, rainbow). Each spirit is called by specific melodies and rhythms; this tradition shows links to Hausa bori spirit-possession practices.

TOUAREG

(Kel TamasheQ) described as migrating south (8th–12th c AD) from Magrehb and carrying Amazigh (BERBER) culture.

Profound attachment to music and poetry linked to Amazigh roots; emphasis on poetry as cultural anchor. Described as nomadic, traveling throughout the Sahel;

Touareg tradition of “vivid and refined courtly love poetry” praising women’s qualities (in Tamashek language). music playable by anyone (no professional caste). Belluwel and akhaguwwen singing techniques documented in 70s recorded releases.
