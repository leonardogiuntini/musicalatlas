Côte d’Ivoire lies across both savanna and forest zones: northern regions in the Voltaic savanna belt; central and southern regions in the forest belt. The Bandama River within Côte d’Ivoire marks a long-term internal cultural boundary between two forest-music regions. The forest belt in general is marked by extreme ethnic fragmentation, small-scale political linkages, strong role of secret societies and age-grade associations.

Regional traditional genres – Baoulé vocal polyphony, Guro "Zaouli" dance and Bete music, gbégbé and masked-dance repertoires (centre); Lobi (Dagaaba) xylophone traditions (northeast), Senoufo initiation/funeral music (north, including poro rhythms), Dan "Ge"mask music and genres like tan “dance song”, also meaning instrumental music/dance, zlee “praise song”, and gbo “funeral lament” (west), and Nzema/Appollo abissa drum-dance traditions (southeast coast) are presented as ongoing, distinctive musical cultures in Côte d’Ivoire.

Tankë ge (“dance ge”) within the broader DAN GE institution perform at harvest celebrations and rites of passage such as weddings and funerals; their music and mask dances are documented in early 1960s recordings.

Abidjan develops as the capital of West Africa’s recording industry and, through the 1970s, as the “musical crossroads” of the region, attracting artists from across the continent.

“Wave of independence” in the 1960s; with Côte d’Ivoire newly independent, BAULE vocal pop built on the gbégbé rhythm artists like Les Soeurs Comöe are heard from transistor radios in every bar. Orchestre de la R.T.I. pop (with singers like Aïcha Koné and Nayanka Bell) blends Ivorian song with influences such as American soul and soft funk. Amédée Pierre’s dopé style uses a strong Congolese rumba flavour and becomes a model for later modern styles. Mamadou Doumbia – 1960s celebrity who tours throughout West Africa and the US;

The 1970s and first 1980s remain a “heady period” for Abidjan’s scene. Ernesto DjéDjé’s ZIGLIBITHY (BETE rhythm base plus contemporary band set-up of drum kit, bass, guitars, horns, backing vocals) is hailed as giving Côte d’Ivoire its first modern musical identity; album “Gnoantre-Ziboté” (1977, recorded in Nigeria) becomes a pan-African hit. Gnaore Djimi’s polihet and Luckson Padaud’s laba laba derive directly from ziglibithy while keeping its traditional Bété spirit. Bailly Spinto – veteran of the local scene who continues to perform modern music strongly rooted in gbégbé. Anoman Brouh Félix, François Lougah and composer Jimmy Hyacinthe (modernizer traditional goly rhythm and producer for Aïcha Koné) are cited as enduring figures from the first generation of modern Ivorian artists. Meiway – Nzema/Appollo musician steeped in Grand-Bassam folk bands; after early success with Les Génitaux, moves to Paris, forms Défense d’Ivoire (prize-winning in 1987) and records “Ayibebou” (1989) with the beginnings of ZOBLAZO style.

From the mid-1980s major labels (Ebony, DC Productions, SID, SACODIS) and big distributors withdraw one by one, reducing Côte d’Ivoire’s role as regional epicenter as studios open in neighboring countries and other markets grow.

REGGAE “heralded” by Ivorian artists Alpha Blondy: is the first on the African scene to use reggae to speak to African urban youth, recording with The Wailers at Tuff Gong in Jamaica and launching a powerful West African reggae movement.Dickaël Liadé creator of Vava music modernizing Bété music.

From 1987 to 1999, the revived ge "Gedro" from Dan village Petit Gbapleu performs constantly around Man, across Côte d’Ivoire and abroad, while dance masks are hired for religious practice, entertainment, and political/economical public-relations events.

Petit Gbapleu Gedro group and associates – including Semlen Aimé (whose dream-inspired revival of Gedro in 1987 leads to a popular touring ge until Aimé’s death in 1999), master drummer Goueu Tia Jean-Claude (a young but highly sought-after drummer), elder singer Goneti, and organizer Gba Gama (Président des jeunes).

Tiken Jah Fakoly (DYULA, forced to flee Côte d’Ivoire and settles in Bamako), together with Ismaël Isaac and Serges Kassy, follows on the reggae scene.

ZOUGLOU – emerges in the early 1990s with the unrivaled leader Didier Bilé amid a university housing crisis in Yopougon; a satirical, ironic dance music with humorous lyrics exposing student hardship, mixing French, pidgin and Baoulé into a new slang, and functioning as protest music for Ivorian youth. It spreads to Burkina Faso and Mali and peaks in the mid-1990s; later leading bands include Magic System, Les Garagistes, Les Poussins Chocs and Soum Bill.

From mid-1990s, hip-hop grows among youth and producers begin recording artists such as All Mighty, Angelo and RAS.

Outbreak of war in September 2002 is described as the “death knell” of the Ivorian music industry; producers estimate 60–70 percent loss of market share and around 80 percent of national recordings being pirated.

Mapouka – the style that overtook zouglou before coupé-décalé took over, indicating another major dance/music craze in the same era.

Coupé-décalé – bursts onto the scene in a war-torn Côte d’Ivoire; in street-kid Noushi slang the term means “get rich quick and run.” It emerges in Paris clubs around 2000 (DJ Jacob at the Nelson) and spreads to other black nightclubs; from 2003, after Douk Saga’s hit “Sagacité,” it becomes massively popular across West Africa and grows to an estimated 40% of Ivorian musical output. Other notable coupé-décalé DJs include DJ Kaloudji (Abidjan), Serpent Noir, DJ Arafat, Innocent Versace and the Résistance DJ collective.

Dobet Gnahoré’s 2004 “Ano Néko” blends urban and traditional textures (electric & acoustic instruments, gourds, balafon, aoko, bells, percussion) with socially engaged lyrics.
