To the west of the Mandinka are the Tukulor, Fulɓe, Wolof, Serer and Jola (Diola)(AKONTING) collectively called Senegambians, whose languages belong to the West Atlantic subfamily.

The savanna social organization for many West African societies is said to follow a stratified system similar to that of the WOLOF, with landed aristocracy, hereditary military class, craft guilds, free peasants, hereditary house servants and slaves.Portuguese presence in Senegal in the 15th century. - First French settlements established in 1659.

Early 19th century: Mande legacy “reinforced” by an influx of griots from Mali after the break-up of the Bamana empires. Alongside jaliyaa, the text implies a contrasting “folk” sphere of dance drumming and singing: all-male drum troupes perform for varied village events, mostly involving women, who are also the main subject of the songs.

Late 19th century: Senegal becomes the administrative base of France’s West African empire.

1960: Léopold Sédar Senghor (first president; poet) as prime negritude exponent; his commitment to culture leads to promotion of griot music via troupes/ensembles. Star Band de Dakar formed by Ibrahim Kassé to play at Senegal’s independence celebrations. Few Senegalese had access to recorded music; dance bands attached to nightclubs serving a small bourgeois elite seeking “sophisticated” Western sounds; outkast Yakhya Fall later forms Star Number One together with Papa Serigne Seck; traditional music stigmatized as “primitive” and “shameful” (linked to caste associations). Cuban and Latin covers dominate; gradual “Africanization” (Cuban songs adapted into Wolof; traditional Wolof/Mandinka songs and rhythms introduced). Djimo Kouyate was an early member of Ensemble Instrumental Traditionnel du Senegal and later founder of Ensemble National des Ballets du Senegal.

Late 60s - Dakar nightclub scene strongly oriented to Cuban/Latin music; Cuban songs adapted into Wolof; traditional songs/rhythms introduced into dance-band settings.

1970: Dakar described as still “in many ways a French city”; Baobab Club created by businessmen/politicians in the Plateau (European quarter) becomes a major cultural node; veteran Star Band saxman Baro Ndiaye recruited guitarist Barthelemy Attisso, singer Rudy Gomis and percussionist Balla Sidibe to form Orchestra Baobab, together with Wolof griot Laye Mboup, bringing neo-Islamic sounds, and Nigerian saxophonist Peter Udo, introducing highlife touch.

Late 1970s: social/cultural balance shifts toward the quartiers populaires; a “pop revolution” emerges around a young Youssou N’Dour and a raw new music called MBALAX. He founds proud Wolof-language pop stardom Etoile de Dakar in 1977, together with El Hadji Faye; addresses youth of a rapidly urbanizing, demographically exploding country. Super Diamono de Dakar: reggae-inspired militancy + jazz cool + Senegalese Sufi mysticism; Abdoulaye Prosper Niang founds Xalam, new thing jazz tastes band, and later works with griot Souleymane Faye;

Early 1980s: N’Dour begins tackling social themes (economic migration, African identity, apartheid), explicitly “expanding the role of the griot” toward social critic/political commentator.

Postwar-to-present thread noted: popular Islam/Sufi brotherhoods’ influence pervades even pop music (noted as a major influence on migrants/urban culture).

Additional “superstar” names join Senegal’s pantheon, like Baaba Maal, Thione Seck, Ismaël Lô, Mansour Seck, Touré Kunda,

First international release by a Senegalese woman recorded in 1989 by griotte Kiné Lam, followed by traditional divas named Daro Mbaye, Madiodio, Khar Mbaye Madiaga. Producers/entrepreneurs include Laminé Faye, Cheikh Tidiane Tall, Papis Konaté;

Mid-1990s: Generation Boul Fale signaled by Alioune Mbaye Nder’s Thiossane nightclub performances; described as a youth attitude toward authority/politics. Omar Pène leads Super Diamono as singer. Baaba Maal’s working process on “Firin’ in Fouta” is anchored in the country’s geography: he returns to his ancestral village Podor in the north, records local indigenous performance there, then moves to Dakar to create studio rhythm tracks, and finally travels to England to complete the project. Malick Pathé Sow, Morikeba Kouyate.

Coumba Gawlo framed as griotte with modern image, commenting on social issues. Fatou Laobé: neo-traditional figure specializing in tassou; tassou described as originally performed after marriages by women of the Laobe woodworking caste (with dancing).

Hip-hop figures and roles: Positive Black Soul (Didier Awadi; Duggy-Tee), Daara J, Pee Froiss (leader Xuman).
