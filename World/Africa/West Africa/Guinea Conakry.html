Guinea is structured into four historically interconnected though culturally distinct regions: Basse Côte, Futa Jallon, Haute Guinée, Guinée Forestière

JALIYAA is framed as a “classical” tradition of the Mande.

Early 20th-century Guinean urban culture sees the GUITAR introduced (probably by soldiers returning from Europe after World War I), becoming a symbol of modernity and replacing the BANJO in Conakry ballroom bands.

In the early 1930s, ethnomusicologist George Herzog undertakes one of the first detailed studies of performance in the Guinea Coast area recorded in wax cylinders.

1950 high-fidelity recordings of MANINKA music from Guinea document early-20th-century forms of MANDE classical music.

Keita Fodeba founds the troupe Les Ballets Africains in Paris in the 1950s, later directed by Kante Facelli. Conakry orchestras such as La Douce Parisette and La Joviale Symphonie serve colonial elites with European and Latin American dances (beguine, waltz, tango, paso doble) plus a few 'Guinean folk' pieces.

Guinea’s independence (1958) turns Les Ballets Africains into a national ensemble, and every nation where the Mande live establishes a similar state-sponsored group. Its 1959 New York debut and later status as Guinea’s national ensemble model how “national” Mande culture can be staged for Western audiences and inspire similar troupes in countries where Mande live. Syli Orchestre National, Bembeya Jazz National, Balla et ses Balladins and Keletigui et ses Tambourinis develop a big-band style rooted in Cuban, Latin and Mande jeli repertoire, with musicians like Grand Papa Diabaté, Sekou Bembeya Diabate and Demba Camara . Women play enduring roles in handing down jalyaa, from Les Amazones de Guinée and female jeli stars like Kadé Diawara and Mama Kanté. Kouyate Sory Kandia, star vocalist from Les Ballets Africains, combines an operatic tenor range with a griot timbre. El Hadj Djeli Sory Kouyaté, a virtuoso balafonist and leader of the Ensemble Concoba, epitomises classical jeli balafon repertoire and virtuosity.

Kulung and the Woni Ensemble are recorded transmitting KPELLE traditions.

In the first 70s Kante Manfila moves to Mali to join Les Ambassadeurs du Motel de Bamako; same for Mory Kanté, joining the Rail Band.

M’Bady et Diaryatou Kouyaté, KORA player and singer couple featured on “Guinée: kora et chant du N’Gabou”, representing leading Guinean exponents of jaliyaa.

In 1984, Guinea's state arts system collapses; General Lansana Conté's regime pursues privatization, and music shifts into a free-market system dominated by private producers, piracy and bootlegging.

Cassette-era Mande pop develops a vedette-centred format: star singers and state-orchestra veterans like Mama Kanté, Kadé Diawara, Kerfala Kanté and Mory Djely Kouyate are backed by synthetic textures instead of large live orchestras, recasting jeliya in romantic, highly produced pop formats. Sekouba Bambino, a jeli from Mali and former Bembeya Jazz singer, becomes Guinea’s most revered vedette. Female jeli like Oumou Dioubaté, Missia Saran Diabate, Sayon Bamba Camara and Maciré Sylla continue jalyaa traditions and fusions. In 1987, Mory Kanté moves into hi-tech global dance music with his 1987 hit song "Yé Ké Yé Ké", which was one of Africa's best-ever selling hits.

Ibro Diabaté’s Paris-produced debut album 'Allah Nana' (early 1990s) is widely praised for its delicate arrangements. Djeli Moussa Diawara.

A hybrid style labelled 'Mande-zouk' emerges in Guinea, blending Antillean zouk beats with local rhythms; artist Fode Baro becomes one of its key exponents.

Late 1990s and early 2000s bring diversification beyond Mande jeli dominance: while some veterans plan institutions such as Mory Kanté, diversified scenes arise.

Fula semi-acoustic pop emphasises flutes and local rhythms: Lama Sidibé becomes a national Fula star with 'Faalaama' (1999), built on thorough research into Futa Jallon traditions; Petit Yero Bantinghel, Binta Laly Sow and Fatou Barry adapt trimmed-down nyamakala (fula jeli) sounds into accessible contemporary pop.

Les Espoirs de Coronthie SUSU 'busker pop' turns the rattling of street instruments into chart hits.

Diasporic Afro-jazz and roots-pop projects rework Guinean traditions, as in Momo Wandel Soumah’s Afro-Swing, Ba Cissoko’s kora-rock and Maciré Sylla’s jazzy Sussu/Mande fusions.

Hip-hop and reggae figures such as Kill Point (seen as godfathers of the scene), Ideal Black Girls and Degg J Force 3 develop Guinean rap, often recording in Dakar’s more advanced studios; Bill de Sam’s rap version of the classic 'Sunjata' unexpectedly gains international airplay.
