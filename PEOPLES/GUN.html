The GBE groups, including FON, GUN, and the EWE of Togo and modern Ghana, trace their common origin to Tado in modern Togo, on the left bank of the river Mono. In the 1500s, disputes within Tado lead to migrations: one section moves west to found Notsie, which becomes the center for Ewe dispersal over the region between the Mono and Volta rivers in Togo and Ghana. The Ewe who move west from this Togo-based center live precariously as a minority within powerful kingdoms of neighboring peoples and evolve more than 120 microstates differing in dialect and cultural traits, while related kin to the east create larger, more centralized units.

Ewe exodus in the late 1600s from the tyrannical king Agokoli of Nots, using fierce drumming to intimidate his warriors during escape. Migrating southwest, Ewe groups found settlements along a large lagoon near the mouth of the Volta River; the group led by Wenya, too tired to go farther, becomes known as the ANLO (cramped). Other Ewe families settle along the Atlantic coast and upland hills of this region, laying the basis for later communities in what became Ghana and Togo.

Hunters, regarded as repositories of knowledge given by God, possessing special herbs and the ability to communicate safely with dangerous animals, conduct traditional customs involving drumming, double bell, and worship, and in legends they are the ones who first bring the forest/monkey dance and drumming that becomes Agbekor (traditional war dance/drumming) back to the community.

The 18th and 19th centuries see frequent military conflicts with neighboring groups, with European traders, and among Ewe themselves; the Anlo-Ewe gain a fearsome reputation as warriors, forming the backdrop for war drumming traditions. Britain, Germany, and France administer Ewe territory during a brief colonial period from the 1880s to the 1950s, after which Ewe people live in the independent nation-states of Ghana and Togo. Christian missionaries whip students for attending traditional performance events, discouraging participation in Ewe music and dance. After Ghanas statehood in 1957, national government sponsors professional troupes in which Agbekor is a staple, contributing to broader Ewe cultural visibility.

Contemporary Ewe traditional music idioms with freshly composed songs, contrasted explicitly with Agbekor as an old repertory inherited from ancestors. Much of Ewe life focuses on dance clubs that function as entertainment, recreation, and ceremonial institutions; Each Ewe dance club has composers who create music in three named genres: axatsevu, akpewu, and specific style pieces characterized by distinctive drumming and dancing.

Funeral music in Anlo communities – dance-club repertories (e.g., Adzomani) performed at funerals; “good music” at funerals is described as the deepest expression of loss, and a funeral without music is lamented as “(what) a sad thing.”

Vodun religion (AJA, EWE, FON): voodoo rhythms, Zangbeto (guardian of the nights).
